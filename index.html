Who we are

We go further into fewer situations.

We're a small firm, and we like it that way. Most advisers hand over a report and move on. Most funds write a cheque and wait. We do neither. We get right inside a business, come to understand it better than anyone on the outside, and then roll up our sleeves. If a company needs a cleaner governance setup, a better capital structure, a stronger management team, or a product it doesn't have yet, our first instinct is to build it, not to write it up in a slide. That's why we only take on a few serious relationships a year and stay with them for years. We're never going to win on volume, and we're not trying to.

What we do

Advise. Invest. Build.

It's one firm doing one thing in three ways. We get inside a business, then give it whatever it needs most: our advice, our capital, or something we build for it.

01

Advise

This is the engine. Ten years of advisory work pays the bills and keeps us close to the market. But the fees aren't really the point. What matters is seeing how a business runs from the inside, and earning the kind of trust that later turns into much bigger things.

02

Invest

This is where we put money to work. When a business is worth owning, we buy in and then advise it as our own. Owning it is what makes the advice actually happen. We look for companies that already generate cash, where the biggest gains come from better structure long before they come from technology.

03

Build

Sometimes we spot the same expensive problem in three different companies, with nothing on the market that fixes it. When that happens, we build the fix ourselves. A small team makes the simplest version that can answer one question: will people use it and pay for it? If the answer is yes, it grows into a company of its own.

There's one rule we don't break: we never send an invoice for something we build. We take a share of it instead, either in the new venture or in the business it makes more valuable.

Our process

We can partner with your business in under 60 days.

We like to keep things simple and we don't fuss over small details. We know the few things that really matter to us, so we won't waste your time. Here's how a partnership usually comes together.

Typical steps

  1. Day 1

    Intro call

    A relaxed 30-minute conversation to learn your story and your values, and to see whether we can help.

  2. Day 2

    Mutual NDA

    We sign a mutual non-disclosure agreement, so both sides can speak freely and everything stays confidential.

  3. Day 3

    Simple info share

    A short list of what we'd like to see — usually revenue detail and high-level financials. Send what you can, when you can.

  4. Day 5

    Letter of intent

    A clear, plain-language term sheet with the valuation and structure. If it works for you, we both sign a letter of intent.

  5. Day 5–55

    Diligence & plan

    We confirm the few things that matter — usually within the first two or three weeks — and build the plan to strengthen the business together.

  6. Day 60

    Sign & partner

    We sign, we fund, and the real work begins. Your team and brand stay; the operator runs the business and we build alongside — for the long term.

The loop

It works as a circle, not a line.

Every stage feeds the one after it, and the last one loops back to the start. The things we build teach us how to give better advice. Better advice wins better clients. Better clients let us see more. And the more we see, the sharper the next thing we build.

  1. 1

    Origination

    The partners. Relationships built over a decade, referrals, and outreach to companies newly caught by the audit rules. Deliberately narrow — a few serious relationships a year, held for years.

  2. 2

    Advisory

    The engine. It funds the firm, keeps our tools sharp, and buys the one thing money can't: a real view of how a business runs and where it is structurally weak.

  3. 3

    Investment

    The position. We acquire businesses worth owning and then advise our own company — the only reliable way to make advice stick. The operator is accountable for the business getting better.

  4. 4

    Research

    The compounding asset. Privileged access becomes something we own: a documented view of where a sector is repeatedly and expensively broken — not what's interesting, but what's broken often enough to be worth solving.

  5. 5

    Ideation

    Naming the gap. The bar: a problem seen in three separate companies that nothing off the shelf solves. One company's problem is a custom job; three companies' problem is a product. Most ideas die here — and that's the point.

  6. 6

    Build & test

    We build the smallest thing that settles the question — will anyone use it and pay for it? The kill number and the graduation trigger are written before the first line of code, and held by the partners.

  7. 7

    Graduation

    A build that passes its test becomes a company in its own right — its own cap table, run by an operator, held in the portfolio. It throws off the cash that funds the next one, and the loop begins again.

This is the reason a pure advisory firm can't work the way we do. It can give advice, but it can't own anything or build for its own account, so what it learns on one job never carries over to the next.

Why now

Vietnam's private economy is getting formal, fast.

This isn't a slow shift. Companies that spent years running on informal books now have to put things on a proper footing, and the window to help them won't stay open forever.

Rising transparency

Presumptive tax is on the way out. Electronic invoicing now reaches even small businesses. And once a company passes a modest size, it's increasingly expected to have an independent audit, often for the very first time.

A cohort seeing itself clearly

A lot of family-owned, unlisted companies are about to see their own numbers clearly for the first time. Many will find that making money and being ready to raise capital, bring in a partner, or pass the business on are three very different things.

A capital gap

Small and mid-sized firms are the bulk of Vietnam's economy, but only a small slice of formal credit reaches them. Most lending still follows collateral rather than cash flow, and once you drop below the size regional private equity looks at, proper structured capital is hard to come by.

Our opening

So you have a whole group of companies becoming more transparent, finding gaps in how they're built, and short of the money to fix them. We've spent ten years building relationships with exactly these companies. That's our opening, and the timing won't last forever.

How we think

Five ideas sit underneath everything else.

01

Advice you can't enforce mostly doesn't happen

A recommendation only becomes a real change when someone owns the outcome. That's why we invest now, instead of just advising.

02

Access is the scarce asset, not capital

Anyone can write a cheque. Very few people get invited inside a Vietnamese business, shown the real numbers, and trusted to handle them.

03

Insight compounds only if you keep it

Firms that give advice and walk away never really build on what they learn. We hold on to it, and turn it into investments and products.

04

Depth beats volume

There are only two of us at the top. We're never going to win on the number of deals, so we win by going deeper into fewer of them than anyone else will.

05

Structure is the lever we know best

Capital structure, governance, reporting, the way people are paid. Technology matters too and we use it, but it's usually the second thing we reach for, not the first.
